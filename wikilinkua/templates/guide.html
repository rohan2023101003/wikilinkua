WikiLinkua User Guide

Learn a new language through the languages you already know. This page is a quick in-site guide.

Documentation hub

  • Docs index

  • Project on GitHub
  • Report issues / give feedback

How to start

  1. Pick the languages you already know.
  2. Pick the target language you want to learn.
  3. Use Home for mixed practice, Words for browsing, Phrases for sentence practice, and Progress to track your review state.

Your progress is stored in the browser on the device you are using.

How words are linked — read this first

Wikidata connects words in several different ways, and they mean very different things. WikiLinkua never blurs them: a shared meaning is not shared history. Every link is labelled with what it actually claims.

  • Cognate: both words descend from the same ancestor word. Found by following "derived from lexeme" (P5191) upwards from both sides until the chains meet — for example a Malayalam and a Punjabi word that both come from the same Persian root.
  • Borrowed from your language: this word was taken directly from a word you know. Also P5191, but a direct line of descent rather than a shared ancestor.
  • Source of your word: the reverse — a word you know came from this one.
  • Same meaning: a word for the same thing exists in a language you know (P5137). This says nothing about the words being related. Most links you will see are of this kind.
  • Related meaning: the meanings are linked as synonym or antonym.
  • False friend: looks like a word you know but means something else.
  • Looks or sounds similar: only the shape or sound resembles a word you know, with no recorded historical link. This is a warning, not a relationship — it may be pure coincidence.

Only the first three claim the words share a history. A word for "animal" that happens to point at the same concept as an English word is a translation, not a cognate.

Other terms you will see

  • Lexeme: Wikidata's entry for a word (id like L1234), holding its spellings, forms and meanings.
  • Sense: one meaning of a lexeme. A word with several meanings has several senses.
  • Gloss: the short meaning label shown for a word.
  • Mapped word: a target-language word whose meaning is linked to a concept (P5137). Only mapped words can be matched across languages, so they are the population every percentage is measured against.
  • Linked word: a mapped word that also has a counterpart in one of your languages.
  • Leitner box: the spaced-repetition level (0–5) that decides when a word comes back. Intervals are 0, 1, 3, 7, 14 and 30 days; a word counts as known from box 3.
  • Sample: for languages with a lot of data, one query cannot return every word, so the browsable list is a sample. The app says so when that happens, and percentages are still counted exactly and separately.
  • Curated false friend: a well-known trap written by hand because Wikidata does not record it yet. These are kept out of all Wikidata-derived counts.

What the numbers mean

  • "Words with a link: X%" — of all target-language words that carry a concept link, this share also has one in a language you know. It is not a claim that you already know that share of the language, and most of those links are "same meaning", not shared origin. The exact counts are shown underneath it.
  • "Box 4/5" on the Progress page is the word's spaced-repetition level, not a percentage of how well you know it — nothing measures that.
  • Evidence counts on the Words page (how many cognates, borrowings and so on) describe the words currently loaded. When the list is a sample, the page says so.

What each practice mode means

  • Flashcards: reveal target words and meanings quickly.
  • Friend or Faux: decide whether a similar-looking pair is a true match or a trap.
  • Recall / Reverse recall: answer from word to meaning, or meaning to word.
  • Odd one out: three words share a recorded history with words you know and one does not. You get one guess, then the answer is shown with the reason.
  • Match pairs: pair words with their meanings against the clock.
  • Phrases: sentence practice built from words you already know or are learning.
  • Video / Article: learn from real Wikimedia media and text, with subtitle- or extract-based flashcards. If none of your languages has subtitles for a video, English is offered as a support language — for many language pairs that is the only thing that makes a video usable.

What the main labels mean

  • New / unseen: you have not answered the word yet.
  • Learning: you have started practicing the word, but it is not mastered yet.
  • Known: the word has reached box 3 or above.
  • Due for review: the word is ready to come back into practice.

A single correct answer does not usually make a word “known”; it normally moves the word up one box at a time.

Choosing languages

The picker lists every language that has words in Wikidata — around 1,560 — with the number of words each one has, because that number is the honest predictor of how much the app can teach you. Languages with very little data are hidden behind “show all”.

A few languages live under a different name in Wikidata than you might expect: Hindi and Urdu words are stored under Hindustani, and Norwegian under Bokmål. Searching for "Hindi" or "Norwegian" will still find them.

Progress is saved per language pair. If you learn some French and then switch to Malayalam, Malayalam starts fresh — and your French is exactly where you left it when you switch back. Nothing is overwritten.

Contributing back

WikiLinkua can only teach what Wikidata knows, so the Contribute page measures your language live and lists what is missing, most important first. Each task says what it unlocks for learners, how many words are affected, and links straight to the words on Wikidata so you can fix them.

  • Words with no meaning recorded, or no concept link — these are invisible to learners.
  • Words with no derived from lexeme statement — the only thing that lets the app show a genuine cognate.
  • Words with no pronunciation or no IPA. You can record a pronunciation yourself: it is published to Wikimedia Commons under your own account and then linked to the word's form with P443, so the word really does gain audio and leaves the list. If a recording already exists on Commons, you can link that instead of uploading.
  • Senses with no example sentence, and words with no inflected forms.
  • Grammatical gender problems — the app checks whether your language actually uses gender before suggesting anything, and flags stray gender statements as likely errors.

Article mode links to Content Translation when an article is missing in your language, and Video mode shows which Commons videos still need subtitles.

How phrases work

Phrases are built from words you already know or are learning. When available, the app prefers human translations from Tatoeba; if none are available, it falls back to Wikimedia MinT.

Phrase review is separate from word review. That means a sentence can come back later even after you have seen it once.

Privacy and access

  • No password is stored by WikiLinkua.
  • Real login uses Wikimedia OAuth and only reads your Wikimedia identity.
  • Pronunciation uploads need Commons upload permission.
  • Learning progress currently stays in your browser unless a future sync layer is added.

Third-party services

  • Wikidata Query Service
  • Tatoeba
  • Wikimedia MinT
  • Wikimedia Commons
  • Wikipedia APIs
  • Wikimedia OAuth

UI style

The UI follows Wikimedia Codex style: blue primary actions, neutral gray cards and borders, clear spacing, and responsive layouts.

Future scope

  • Cross-device progress sync
  • Better explanations in the app
  • More detailed progress and streak views
  • Richer pronunciation practice
  • More language coverage and content sources
